# Writing rules for this project

<!-- maintainer: bump the version string below on every release (see README "Maintainer notes") -->
These rules apply to all prose in this repository. They were generated by `/ai-slop:writing` from the [ai-slop-skill](https://github.com/se-uhd/ai-slop-skill) (skill version 2026-10_rev2) and combine the writing rules maintained by the [Software Engineering Group at Heidelberg University](https://github.com/se-uhd) with a general AI trope catalog from [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by [ossama.is](https://ossama.is).

Edit this file freely to add project-specific conventions. The sections below are a starting point. Once you edit them, this file is yours.

To audit a draft against everything below, use the `ai-slop:review` skill (or `ai-slop:review-diff` to scope the audit to git-modified lines). In Claude Code, invoke `/ai-slop:review`. In Codex, select the skill through `/skills` or a `$` mention.

## nnterp site conventions

These rules are specific to the pages under `site/docs/`. They come before the general rules below; where the two disagree, these win.

### Who reads

A researcher who has written an nnsight trace and wants to know what nnterp adds. Assume `with model.trace(...)`, `.save()`, `.input`, `.output`, invokes, and `tracer.iter` are known. Link to [nnsight.net](https://nnsight.net) for them. Never re-explain them.

### Page shape

- A page answers one question, and its title states it. The first paragraph gives the answer in one or two sentences, and a runnable block follows. No "Overview", "What this is for", or "Introduction" section. No paragraph that says what the page will cover.
- No closing summary, "Next steps", or "See also" section. Where one page follows another, end with a single sentence that links it.
- Headings are the nouns or questions a reader would search for. At most two levels below the title.
- One example per point. A second variant goes into one sentence of prose or is dropped.
- A Guide page stays under about 150 lines of prose and code together. Split by question, never by size.
- Index pages route. They hold one line per page, saying what the page answers, and nothing else.

### Code

- Every concept is shown by a block that runs as written: the imports, the load, the trace, the print, and the printed output as a trailing comment. Run it before writing the page and paste the real output. There is no runner, so the author is the check.
- Use `openai-community/gpt2` unless the page is about something GPT-2 lacks. Then use the smallest public checkpoint that has it, and name it once.
- Prose around a block says what the block shows and what would differ elsewhere. It does not narrate the block line by line.
- Link to the Documentation tab for signatures. Do not repeat a signature in prose.

### Caveats, families, and admonitions

- Where a value behaves differently on some families, say so in one sentence at the point where it matters, and name the families. Do not collect caveats into a list at the end of a page, and do not catalog every family. The encyclopedia does that.
- At most one admonition per page, and only for something that silently gives a wrong result. None for notes, tips, or reminders.
- No bold runs, no emoji, and no icons in prose. Bold a term only where it is defined.
- Tables are for tabular facts, such as a value, its shape, and its meaning. Not for prose.

### Terms

- Use the glossary's terms exactly. A *family* is the module for one `model_type`. A *checkpoint* is one Hub repository. A *value* is a standard value such as `layer_output`. A *name* is an entry in the vocabulary such as `layers[i].self_attn`.
- Code identifiers go in code font. The things they denote go in words: `layer_output` is the residual stream leaving a block.

### Nothing environment-specific, nothing historical

- No hostnames, personal paths, conda environments, GPU names, or deployment details.
- State the current behavior only. No "as of 0.8", "previously", "after the refactor", and no issue numbers or talk of open bugs.
- Version-specific notes survive only where the difference still bites a reader, such as the transformers version a page was run against.

### Source material

The pages under `docs/` in the repository are source material: facts, caveats, and snippets to check. Take facts from them, verify each against the code, and run every snippet. Do not copy their structure, their front matter, or their section template.

## Apply the rules in context

- **Judge the passage before changing it** (`G.context-first`). Apply stylistic rules as diagnostics, not as automatic replacements. Read the surrounding paragraph and identify what the sentence contributes before proposing an edit. A word match, a trope label, or a count above a threshold is a reason to inspect, not proof that the prose needs changing. A stylistic finding needs a concrete explanation of what is unclear, misleading, inflated, redundant, or obstructive to reading, and why the replacement improves it. Preserve the sentence's purpose, logical relations, conceptual distinctions, emphasis, scope, evidential strength, and the author's voice. An abstraction can be the right level of description, a familiar metaphor can clarify a relation, and a transition can do useful argumentative work. Do not replace a statement of purpose with a summary of topics, unpack a summary that the following sentences already explain, or remove a contrast that distinguishes competing claims. Prefer the smallest change that addresses the demonstrated problem. Leave the original when the alternative is merely shorter, more literal, or more explicit without a clear benefit. This principle governs stylistic directives and their self-checks throughout the layers and the trope catalog. Explicit user choices and concrete conventions such as spelling, terminology, citation syntax, and statistical reporting still apply; distinguish a convention correction from a claim that the prose is unclear or machine-written.

## Language

- **Use American English consistently** (`G.american-english`). That means analyze (not analyse), behavior (not behaviour), organization (not organisation), modeling (not modelling), color (not colour), favor (not favour), center (not centre), defense (not defence), and license (not licence). Check for British spellings introduced by collaborators or spell-checkers set to the wrong locale.
- **Treat "data" as singular** (`G.data-singular`). "The data shows" and "the data is," not "the data show" or "the data are." Style guides accept both forms, and this project uses the singular consistently.
- **Use "such as" not "like" to introduce examples** (`G.such-as`). Write "scenarios such as code completion," not "scenarios like code completion." `Like` is colloquial in this construction. `Such as` is the more formal register. Apply this even when the original prose already uses `like`. Do not carry it over verbatim into a rewrite. `Like` remains correct as a verb ("readers like terse prose") and in deliberate similes.

## Restricted words (use sparingly)

**Restricted words** (`G.restricted-words`). Legitimate words that AI overuses. Each use should be intentional. If a simpler word works, use it. More than 2 to 3 restricted words in a single paragraph prompts a closer look, not an automatic rewrite. Keep a word that is apt in context or part of an established term.

| Restricted | Preferred alternative(s) | When the restricted form is acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| utilize | use | Only when the distinction from "use" matters |
| leverage | use, apply, employ | Only when the lever metaphor is intentional |
| facilitate | enable, support, help, allow | n/a |
| foster | encourage, support, promote | n/a |
| streamline | simplify, reduce, speed up | Acceptable in process-improvement contexts |
| underscore | emphasize, highlight, stress | n/a |
| harness | use, apply, employ | n/a |
| encompass | include, cover, span | n/a |
| navigate | handle, manage, deal with | Only for literal movement through a UI, website, menu, or file tree (e.g., "navigate the codebook"), never as a metaphor for handling difficulty ("navigate the challenges" -> "handle the challenges") |
| landscape | field, area, environment, situation | n/a |
| nuanced | detailed, subtle, qualified | n/a |
| multifaceted | complex, varied, many-sided | n/a |
| intricate | complex, detailed, elaborate | n/a |
| meticulous | careful, thorough, rigorous | n/a |
| transformative | n/a | Only when genuinely describing transformation |

**Standard vocabulary (e.g., novel, robust, comprehensive, insights, mitigate, enhance, innovative, paradigm, framework, stakeholder, fundamentally, inherently)** is not restricted. Do not overuse it. If a simpler word works equally well, prefer it.

**Transition words (e.g., moreover, furthermore, notably, conversely, crucially)** are also standard and not restricted. Do not overuse them. If more than two appear in a single paragraph, check whether any can be dropped without losing logical flow. The connectives that mark a consequence need a further check, because AI text uses one of them for nearly every consequence rather than overusing the set (see **Consequence connectives** (`G.consequence-connectives`)).

**Consequence connectives** (`G.consequence-connectives`). AI text uses one word for nearly every consequence, and which word depends on the register. General prose concentrates on "so", and academic prose on "therefore" and "thus". The concentration rather than any single use is the tell, and the same test applies to whichever word a document uses most. At the head of a sentence, "So" is the spoken answer opener ("So, the counts differ because the parser skips blank lines"), carries no meaning in writing, and is deleted (see **No formulaic section openings** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`)). Sentence-initial "Therefore," and "Thus," are correct but heavy, and mid-position is lighter ("The counts are therefore unreliable"). Between clauses, ask first whether a connective is needed at all, and delete it where the order of the content already carries the relation. Where one is needed, match it to the step: "so" for a small step in a plain register, therefore or hence to mark a conclusion drawn from evidence just given, thus for a consequence that follows by construction, and consequently or as a result for an outcome that the text then discusses. Where the cause reads better after the effect, recast with because or since ("the counts differ because the parser skips blank lines"). Substituting one connective for another is the last fix rather than the first, and the substitutes count toward the transition-word limit above. Count the family together. More than two or three per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags a text that signposts every step, and at or above that density a document that uses one word for nearly all of them shows the habit. Consecutive sentences that all use the same connective show it at a smaller scale. Parallelism across items of the same kind stays, as in one sentence per exit code or per condition. This rule governs the consequence relation, which is distinct from **Name the relation with a connective** (`G.connectives`) (example and restatement). The intensifier ("so few cases survived"), the purpose connective ("so that a reviewer can check the quote"), the correlative ("so large that"), and quoted material are separate constructions and do not count.

**Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`). The runtime-fetched trope catalog names patterns rather than a closed word list, and several of its entries overlap with these rules. Its **"Delve" and friends** entry names "robust" in the same family as "utilize", "leverage", "streamline", and "harness", where this layer leaves robust unrestricted and lists the other four as restricted words with alternatives. Its **Signposted conclusion** entry flags every "In summary" and "In conclusion", where **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`) permits them in a genuine summary position. Its **Rule of Three pattern** and **Bold-first bullets** entries overlap **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) and **No excessive bold or formatting** (`G.no-excess-bold`), which carry the exceptions. Where the catalog and a layer rule disagree, the layer rule wins. The catalog supplies candidates to inspect, and these rules state the deliberate exceptions. Apply **Judge the passage before changing it** (`G.context-first`) to every candidate, regardless of its catalog status. Frequency ratings prioritize inspection; they do not establish a defect in an individual passage.

## Phrases to avoid

**Phrases to avoid** (`G.phrases-to-avoid`). Stock formulations that read as AI filler or stilted register. Replace each with the plain alternative. The trope catalog (fetched separately) covers the broader set of named patterns. This section holds project-specific additions.

- **"worked example(s)"** -> "example(s)". The qualifier "worked" rarely adds information and reads as padded pedagogical register. Keep it only in the narrow technical sense of a fully solved problem walked through step by step (a textbook "worked solution"), where the contrast with an unsolved exercise is the point.
- **"earn its keep" / "earn its place" / "pull its weight"** -> state what the element contributes, as in "the opener earns its keep" -> "the opener introduces the list that follows". The idiom states a plain judgment as figurative filler.
- **"headline numbers" / "headline figures"** -> name the figures plainly (e.g., "the largest claims", "the most-cited figures", or just "the figures"). "Headline" imports a journalistic, attention-grabbing register that editorializes the numbers instead of stating them.
- **"arm" for a condition, group, variant, or split** -> "condition" or "treatment" for an experimental manipulation, "group" for a set of subjects, "variant" or "configuration" for a version of a prompt, model, or pipeline, and "split" for a data partition. An arm is a group of subjects randomly assigned to one intervention in a trial, or one option in a bandit. Keep the word for those two designs only.
- **"famously" / "notoriously" / "as is well known" / "a classic"** -> state the claim and name its source, or drop the appeal, as in "the tool is famously slow" -> "the tool took 40 minutes per build in our benchmark". The phrase borrows the weight of consensus without supplying evidence, and it marks the claim that most needs a citation. The reader's assumed prior knowledge is the same unnamed authority as "experts argue".

## Terminology consistency

- **Use one term for one concept** (`G.one-term`). Once you introduce a term for a concept, use that same term throughout the document. Do not alternate between synonyms for variety. What reads as elegant variation in literary writing creates ambiguity in technical writing. If "code review" is the term, do not switch to "code inspection," "review process," and "code assessment" in subsequent paragraphs.
- **Define terms on first use, then reuse exactly** (`G.define-once`). If a concept needs a definition or explanation, provide it once. After that, the established term carries the meaning without re-explanation.
- **Synonyms are acceptable only for genuinely different concepts** (`G.distinct-synonyms`). If two terms refer to distinct concepts, use both, but make the distinction explicit.

## Reference

- **Anchor sentence-initial pronouns** (`G.anchor-pronouns`). Check whether the intended noun or preceding claim is easy to identify in the immediate context. Name the antecedent when competing readings, distance, or a change of topic makes it unclear, as in "The compiler emits a warning when the cache is stale. It is disabled by default." -> "The warning is disabled by default." A clause-sized antecedent can take a head noun when that makes the connection clearer: "The parser accepts unterminated strings. This causes silent data loss." -> "This leniency causes silent data loss." A pronoun that clearly refers to the immediately preceding claim can stay. Do not force noun repetition solely because a sentence begins with *This* or *It*. Exempt: dummy *it* ("it is unclear whether", "it follows that") and a demonstrative that already carries its noun ("this study").
- **A summarizing noun must name something that the text has listed** (`G.summarizing-nouns`). *such tools*, *this approach*, *these challenges* after a passage that names no tools, approach, or challenges refer to a category that the text never introduced. Use the noun for what was listed, or introduce the category, as in "LLMs can generate unit tests, triage bug reports, and summarize pull requests. Such tools are already in industrial use." -> "Tools for these tasks are already in industrial use." Same test as above.
- **Definite article only for known referents** (`G.definite-article`). A first mention takes *a/an*, the zero article for mass and plural nouns, or an explicit introduction. *The* is for something already mentioned, unique in context, or entailed by something mentioned. To test it, ask "which one?". If the text so far cannot answer, drop the definite, as in "He built a test generator and the rerun infrastructure to detect flaky tests." -> "He built a test generator and infrastructure to rerun the generated tests and detect which are flaky." A purpose or description after the noun ("the tooling to support X") does not by itself identify it. Exempt: postmodifiers that single out one referent ("the version we used"), nouns unique by nature or convention ("the compiler", "the Linux kernel"), and generic definites ("the developer sees a warning").
- **Keep stand-in references clear** (`G.stand-ins`). A word that stands for a noun or clause (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *the same*, *respectively*, *do so*) can make the reader search for its referent. Replace it when the immediate context leaves competing readings or requires a burdensome lookup. For example, "detect flaky tests among the generated ones" can become "detect which of the generated tests are flaky", and "precision and recall were 0.81 and 0.64, respectively" can become "precision was 0.81 and recall 0.64". Keep a clear reference when spelling it out adds repetition without improving comprehension. A reference to the preceding clause or sentence is not a defect merely because its noun could be repeated.

## Voice

- **Prefer active voice** (`G.active-voice`). Use passive only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when passive reads better. If a sentence works in active voice, use active voice. As a secondary signal, a paragraph in which more than one-third of the sentences are passive is worth a second pass.
- **Use consistent tense within and across adjacent paragraphs** (`G.consistent-tense`). Unmotivated tense shifts are distracting. Switch tense only when the temporal frame changes.
- **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). Repeated present-participle openings ("-ing, ...") can make prose monotonous or obscure who acts. More than one per page is a prompt to inspect their effect, not a quota. Keep an opening that expresses the relation clearly; revise repetitions when they impede the passage.

## Clause boundaries

- **Keep *that* in formal prose** (`G.keep-that`). Omitting the relativizer of a restrictive object relative ("the metrics the benchmark reports") or the complementizer after a reporting verb ("the results show the effect persists") is grammatical but conversational. Formal prose keeps it, and AI text drops it. Scan for two noun phrases in a row with nothing between them, and for a verb that can take a direct object (e.g., show, find, confirm, report, reveal, suggest, indicate, ensure, mean, assume, argue, conclude) followed by a noun phrase that turns out to be a clause subject, which the reader first parses as the object. Restore *that*, or *who* for people, as in "the metrics that the benchmark reports" and "the results show that the effect persists." Exempt: fixed heads ("the way we", "the reason we", "the first time we"), quoted text, and deliberately informal text (commit messages, code comments). Non-restrictive *which* is never omitted and needs no check.
- **Rewrite *whose* clauses on things** (`G.whose-on-things`). AI text states a condition on a property of a thing as a restrictive *whose* clause ("entries whose keys appear in the new citations"). Restate it with *with* ("a model whose weights are public" -> "a model with public weights"), with a participle or verb on the head ("entries whose keys appear in the new citations" -> "entries cited in the new text"), or with the property as the subject of its own clause ("skip a finding whose quote cannot be located" -> "skip a finding if its quote cannot be located"). Use the restatement when it loses nothing. Keep *whose* for a person or group ("authors whose papers were retracted") and where every restatement is longer or repeats the head noun. More than one per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose is a secondary signal.

## Punctuation

- **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Judge each dash on its own. Is an em-dash the right mark, or is it standing in for a period or comma that would read better? Reserve it for a genuine interruption or an appositive that needs setting off. If splitting into two sentences or using a comma works, do that, and never swap an em-dash for parentheses, since both signal a sentence with too many ideas. Count is only a secondary signal. More than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance and is worth a second pass, but one dash used wrongly is a worse problem than three used well. Two structural uses are legitimate and are not over-reliance: (a) appositives that already contain commas, where outer commas around the appositive would be ambiguous (`Three reviewers — Alice, Bob, and Carol — flagged the issue`); and (b) a parenthetical that itself contains parentheses, where em-dashes stand in for an outer pair of parens to avoid nesting (`the corpus — built from public repositories (GitHub, GitLab) — covers ...`). Em-dashes inside quoted source material likewise do not count, since they belong to the quotation, not to the writer. Nor do glyphs that are not em-dashes, such as en dashes in number or page ranges (`pp. 12–18`), minus signs in math, and hyphens or `--` sequences inside code listings.
- **Literal em-dash glyphs in source** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). A literal `—` (U+2014) in code-edited source is a tell on its own. Editors don't autocorrect `-`/`--` into `—`, so humans type `-`, `--`, or `:` (and `---` in LaTeX). Flag every literal `—` in Markdown, plain text, comments, or `.tex`, and replace it with the format's ASCII form, which is `---` in LaTeX. A `—` inside a quotation or a code listing stays. The common case is a uniform `term — gloss` list separator (e.g., API references, glossaries). Keep the list and replace the glyph. Prefer `term: gloss`, a hyphen, or a table. This rule is about the glyph, not the per-mark judgment in **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) above. Converting the glyph is not by itself a fix for the dash. The per-mark test and the density signal apply to any mark used in place of a dash, so `--`, a spaced hyphen, and LaTeX's `---` count the same as `—`.
- **Colons** (`G.colons`). Judge each colon. It is appropriate before a list, a definition, or a deliberate setup-and-payoff that justifies the pause, not as a generic mid-sentence pause. Use a period when it clarifies the relation without losing the colon's setup or emphasis. Watch for a colon followed by a list in every other paragraph, and for colons used in place of em-dashes or parentheses that a rule forbade. Count is a secondary signal. More than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance. Headings, table captions, and `figure:`-style labels are exempt. The rule applies to running prose.
- **Colon before a list or continuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). When a sentence introduces an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), or a paragraph that grammatically completes or elaborates the introducer, end the introducer with `:`, not `.`. AI text systematically defaults to `.` here. To test an introducer, look at what follows. If the following block is a list or directly continues the introducer, use `:`. If it stands as an independent sentence on its own topic, the period is correct. Colons that end an introducer in this way are exempt from the colon count above, which targets mid-sentence colons used as filler pauses, em-dash substitutes, or decorative setup.
- **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). In running prose, capitalize the first word after a colon when what follows is a complete sentence (an independent clause). Keep it lowercase when the colon introduces a fragment, phrase, single word, or list. Examples: "We adopt one rule: Capitalize after a colon when a full sentence follows." vs. "We considered three options: speed, accuracy, and cost." Headings and `figure:` / `table:` labels are exempt. The capitalization is a project convention. AI prose reliably defaults to lowercase here regardless of clause completeness. When a colon introduces an enumerated series rather than a single payoff clause, treat it as a list and keep the first word lowercase even if the items are themselves independent clauses. Prefer splitting a series of three or more clauses into sentences (see **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`)), which removes the question.
- **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Judge each semicolon. It is appropriate to join two closely related independent clauses where the connection adds meaning that a period would lose, or to separate items in a complex list where commas would create ambiguity. It is not a generic mid-sentence pause or a default substitute for a period when both clauses can stand alone. If it could be replaced by a period with no loss, use the period. Two separate sentences are often more natural. The most common misuse is a semicolon between two independent clauses, often before a second clause that opens with a pronoun (e.g., we, it, this, they, these). Default to a period there. Count is a secondary signal. More than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
- **Name the relation with a connective** (`G.connectives`). When a flagged em-dash or colon is introducing an example or a restatement, the precise fix is usually the connective that names that relation, not a bare period or comma. Use an example connective (such as, e.g., for example, including) when what follows illustrates, and a restatement connective (i.e., that is, in other words, namely) when what follows renames, defines, or restates the same content in different words, as in "Some checks run before the build: linting and type checking." -> "Some checks, such as linting and type checking, run before the build." and "The parser is strict — it rejects any unknown key." -> "The parser is strict, that is, it rejects any unknown key." These connectives are standard in academic prose and under-used by AI text, which defaults to a dash or colon instead, so naming the relation both sharpens the meaning and removes a tell. First, apply it only when the mark genuinely introduces an example or a restatement. A dash or colon that sets up a single payoff clause (see **Colons** (`G.colons`)) or introduces a true list is neither an example nor a restatement, and stays as is. Second, keep the choice consistent with the parenthetical-list rules below: Pick one example signal (`e.g.,` or `such as`) and stay with it, and do not pair `e.g.,` with a trailing `etc.`
- **Combined pause-mark count** (`G.pause-mark-count`). Em-dashes, colons, and semicolons all create mid-sentence pauses, and AI text shifts the load between them when any single mark is restricted, so judging each mark alone misses the pattern. The primary test stays per mark. Is each pause the right choice? As a combined secondary signal, a page near all three individual ceilings at once (say 3 em-dashes, 2 colons, 2 semicolons) is over-punctuated even though no single ceiling is exceeded. Around 5 or fewer per page-equivalent of running prose is a reasonable target. For each mark, ask whether a split into two sentences reads better.
- **Sentence length** (`G.sentence-length`). Let sentence length follow the argument and vary where that helps the reader. Three consecutive sentences within 5 words of each other in length are a candidate for checking rhythm, not a requirement to revise one. Keep useful parallelism and sentences that already read naturally. Do not split a sentence solely to lower its word count, vary one solely to change a metric, or homogenize sentence length when editing.
- **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`). AI text over-hyphenates noun-noun stacks placed before another noun ("code-generation benchmarks", "code-translation performance", "prompt-format sensitivity", "data-collection process", "model-evaluation pipeline"). These three-noun stacks are usually unambiguous without the hyphen ("code generation benchmarks"). Drop it. Keep the hyphen only when the unhyphenated form invites a real misread, typically when one element is a participle that could re-attach as a verb ("instruction-following ability" stays hyphenated because "instruction following ability" briefly reads as "instruction following" + "ability" with "following" as a verb). Genuine compound adjectives where one element is an adjective or participle (e.g., high-quality, well-known, large-scale, fine-grained) keep the hyphen. This rule adjusts the hyphen of a legitimate compound only. An invented compound such as "review-flattening" is a coinage to rewrite, not a hyphen to drop (see **No invented compounds or verbs-as-nouns** (`G.no-coinages`)).
- **Oxford comma** (`G.oxford-comma`). Use it, but do not let comma-heavy constructions substitute for clearer sentence structure.

## Structure

- **No formulaic section openings** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`). Never open a section or subsection with "In today's...", "In the realm of...", "In an era of...", "As we explore...", or "[Topic] has become increasingly...". Start with the substance. The same applies to an opening that announces what follows instead of delivering it ("Two constraints shape the design.", "The more important point is where the decision sits.", a paraphrase of the question being answered). Open with the constraint, or with the point, as in "Two constraints shape the design. The first is latency." -> "The design has to keep latency under 50 ms."
- **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`). Do not use "Overall, ...," "In summary, ...," or "In conclusion, ..." to close a single paragraph by restating what it already said. These phrases are acceptable in the following uses: "In summary" and "In conclusion" in actual summary or conclusion sections; "Overall" when synthesizing across multiple data points or conditions (e.g., "Overall, the pattern held across all three groups"); and "In summary, ..." when introducing an enumerated list that consolidates several preceding paragraphs of prose into a checklist (e.g., "In summary, our recommendation is to report: (1) X; (2) Y; (3) Z."). The diagnostic is whether the phrase consolidates content that the reader has not yet seen in list form. If the next construct is a numbered or bulleted list distilling earlier prose, the opener is justified. If it introduces a sentence or two that just restates the paragraph above, it is filler.
- **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). A paragraph normally contains more than one sentence. Avoid the AI tic of breaking prose into one-sentence paragraphs for manufactured emphasis. A sentence fragment set as its own paragraph ("Flaky tests. A twenty-minute build." -> "The tests are flaky, and the build takes twenty minutes.") is the same tic in a shorter form, and none of the structural exceptions below cover it. A one-sentence paragraph is acceptable when it serves a clear structural purpose, such as opening a section with a framing or topic sentence, introducing an enumerated list or figure, marking a section transition, or carrying a topic sentence that stands alone. The diagnostic is whether absorbing the paragraph into its neighbor would lose something that the reader needs. Flag any page with more than one or two single-sentence paragraphs, or any run of three or more consecutive single-sentence paragraphs.
- **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`). Do not reflexively group items in threes. If there are two items, list two. If there are four, list four.
- **Announce a count only when the reader tracks it** (`G.no-announced-counts`). Do not state how many items follow when what follows is continuous prose ("Two constraints shape the design.", "There are three reasons for this.", "We discuss five aspects of the design."). Delete the number and state the first item, as in "There are three reasons for this. The first is cost." -> "Cost is one reason." Keep the count where the reader has to check each item against it: an enumerated specification or checklist; the conditions, phases, or research questions of a study; and the contribution list that a venue expects. To test it, look for a later sentence that refers back to the number ("the third of these", "both of these constraints"). If none does, the number is decoration. This rule governs whether the count is announced, which is distinct from **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) (how many items there are).
- **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`). Examples introduced by "e.g.,", "such as", "including", or numbered markers such as "(1) ... (2) ... (3) ..." should illustrate, not exhaustively iterate. Two or three representative items are almost always enough. If the full set matters, use a table, figure, or dedicated list. Do not pack it into a parenthetical or running sentence. When in doubt, cut the enumeration to the clearest examples and stop.
- **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Do not flatten three or more independent clauses into one sentence as a run-in series. The usual pattern is a colon or dash opening a chain of semicolon-joined clauses, each often carrying its own citation or parenthetical, so that one sentence carries what should be several and the density reads as a list crammed into prose to avoid a line break. Break the chain into separate sentences (a plain sequence, or `First, ... Second, ... Third, ...` when the count itself matters). Only when the items reduce to short parallel fragments, keep the colon so it introduces a true list ("three options: speed, accuracy, and cost"). Two tightly linked independent clauses joined by a semicolon are fine, and a colon or dash introducing a single payoff clause is fine. The target is a sentence that joins three or more clauses, not the punctuation mark. This rule governs joining a list of clauses into one grammatical sentence, which is distinct from **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`) (illustrative-list length) and **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) (item count).
- **No enumerated list disguised as paragraphs** (`G.no-prose-listicle`). Do not open consecutive paragraphs with ordinals that number an unmarked list ("The first wall is the absence of a scoped API... The second wall is the lack of delegated access... The third wall is..."). Either set the material as a real list, or drop the ordinal scaffolding and open each paragraph with its own claim, as in "The first wall is the absence of a scoped API." -> "The service offers no API with scoped access." In a research article, where the venue's prose conventions rule out a bulleted list, the second fix is the only one. Ordinals inside a single paragraph are a different matter and stay. The short `First, ... Second, ...` sequence is the fix that **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`) prescribes. The scale is the test. Ordinals marking a sequence that the reader holds in view are structure, and the same markers opening paragraph after paragraph are an unmarked list.
- **Mark parenthetical lists as examples or exhaustive** (`G.parenthetical-lists`). A bare parenthetical with 2+ comma-separated items reads ambiguously. The reader cannot tell whether the list is representative or complete. Open it with "e.g.," (examples) or "i.e.," (definition or full enumeration), or restructure. When neither is clear and the list is not obviously exhaustive, use "e.g.,". The same applies to inline lists in running text introduced by nothing. Use "such as" or "including." Exception: an abbreviation expansion (e.g., "large language models (LLMs)") or a parenthetical that names a single concept.
- **One example signal per document** (`G.one-example-signal`). Don't mix `e.g.,` with `etc.` in the same parenthetical. "(e.g., a, b, etc.)" is redundant. Write "(e.g., a, b)". Across a document, prefer `e.g., ...` at the start over `..., etc.` at the end and apply the choice consistently.
- **No excessive bold or formatting** (`G.no-excess-bold`). Do not bold phrases for emphasis in running text. Reserve bold for subsection-level headers or matching the document's existing conventions. In a list, a bold lead-in is acceptable when it names the item, as the term being defined or the name of a rule does. It is a formatting tic when it bolds the opening words of each item's sentence, which is how most AI-written documentation formats its lists.
- **Refer back instead of repeating** (`G.refer-back`). State each definition, point, or finding once, in the place where it belongs, and refer back to it from elsewhere rather than restating it. Brief one-sentence reminders are acceptable when needed to follow an argument. Full paragraphs of restatement are not. A reader who needs the information again should be pointed to where it is stated, not given a second copy.
- **Cut padding at the sentence level** (`G.sentence-padding`). Prefer the shortest phrasing that preserves meaning and precision. Sentence-level padding is distinct from vagueness (`G.be-concrete`), inflated vocabulary (`G.restricted-words`), and duplication (`G.refer-back`). A phrase can be concrete, plain, and unique yet still spend more words than its content needs. Replace multi-word filler with its shorter equivalent ("in order to" -> "to", "due to the fact that" -> "because", "in the event that" -> "if", "has the ability to" -> "can", "the majority of" -> "most", "at this point in time" -> "now"). Delete empty lead-ins ("it is important to note that", "it should be mentioned that", "as can be seen"). Cut redundant doublets where one word already implies the other ("end result" -> "result", "future plans" -> "plans", "completely eliminate" -> "eliminate"). The diagnostic is subtractive. If deleting a word or clause costs no meaning, emphasis, or precision, delete it.
- **Cut padding at the paragraph level** (`G.paragraph-padding`). A paragraph should make its point in as many sentences as it needs and no more. Remove windup sentences that announce what the same paragraph then does, and any sentence that restates its predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, or evidence. A paragraph that opens a section is not windup when it states the section's claim or purpose, or when it orients the reader across the subsections with something that the headings do not state (e.g., the order, what the parts share, a definition they all need). It is windup when it merely lists the subsection headings or repeats content stated elsewhere (see **Refer back instead of repeating** (`G.refer-back`)). A paragraph that takes five sentences to deliver one sentence of content is padded, even when every sentence is individually clean. Padding is the opposite failure from the **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`) rule above, which guards against paragraphs cut too short for manufactured emphasis. Generic-truth sentences, true of any document on the subject ("Software testing is a critical part of software development"), aphorisms written to be quoted rather than to advance the argument ("Every metric that rewards volume punishes leverage"), and evaluative sentences that grade the preceding claim without adding to it ("This is an important finding") are legitimate in small numbers and filler in quantity. One such sentence can open a section or mark that a result matters. Flag a paragraph with more than one, or a page-equivalent (~350 words) with more than two or three in total. Where the paragraph already states the claim, fold the grade into it, as in "Build times doubled. This is an important finding." -> "Build times doubled, the largest change that we measured."
- **Reformulate, do not delete** (`G.reformulate`). When prose is flagged, the default fix is to rewrite it into a clear, correct form that keeps the author's intended content. Delete only genuine filler (the padding, windup sentences, and non-evidence-based hedges the rules above target) or when the author explicitly asks for a cut. A flagged claim, example, or qualification carries information that the author chose to include. Resolving the flag by deleting it discards that information silently.

## Tone

- **Match the existing document's voice** (`G.match-voice`). Read the draft before writing. Mirror its register, its level of formality, and how it handles transitions.
- **Be concrete** (`G.be-concrete`). A generic head noun (thing, stuff, aspect, element, factor, area, item, component, piece, part, issue, concept, entity), sometimes behind a vague quantifier (a number of, various, certain, several), needs attention when the reader needs a specific referent and the context does not establish one. An abstract noun is not an empty placeholder merely because it names a category. Keep abstractions that state the purpose, scope, theoretical framing, or relation between claims, and summaries that the surrounding sentences explain. To test one, write the specific noun in its slot: "we changed three things" -> "we changed the retry interval and the cache key". Where the text cannot supply that noun, the blank is the finding rather than the word. Record it under author judgment with the candidate readings and leave the sentence alone, because a guessed noun states something that the author did not. An empty verb phrase takes the same test ("provides valuable insights into test flakiness" -> "shows that most flaky tests depend on timing"). In descriptions of revisions, do not use "moved" or "shifted" as vague substitutes for a change. Name the operation or the resulting behavior: a file was renamed, a default changed, support was added, or a requirement was removed. For example, "the requirements shifted in v2" -> "v2 requires Python 3.14", when that requirement is established by the source. Do not infer a specific change from vague wording. Describe current behavior without version history unless that history helps the reader compare versions, upgrade, reproduce a result, or understand a change. Keep "moved" for an actual relocation with a named source and destination, such as "the configuration file moved from the project root to config/". Physical movement and established technical senses, such as a bit shift, also stay. Keep the generic noun where the generality is the claim: a contrast with another category ("a noun that names a thing rather than an action"), an established technical sense (an XML element, a factor in factor analysis), or a category that the text has defined. A generic noun pointing back at a passage that listed nothing is **A summarizing noun must name something that the text has listed** (`G.summarizing-nouns`). No density signal applies, because the same words are ordinary technical vocabulary.
- **Hedge only from evidence** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). "The data suggests X" is appropriate when the data is ambiguous. "One could argue that X" is filler unless you then explain who argues it and why. As a secondary signal, more than two hedges in one paragraph (e.g., may, might, could, perhaps, to some extent) flag the paragraph. Check that evidence supports each hedge, and remove the hedges that no evidence supports.
- **Take positions** (`G.take-positions`). When the evidence points one direction, say so. Do not present artificial balance.
- **No author-voice metacommentary in published prose** (`G.no-metacommentary`). Asides such as "reader beware," "the reader is warned," "we shall see," "spoiler alert," or "(more on this below)" break the formal register and address the reader directly about the act of reading. Remove them from rendered body text, or move them into editorial notes that do not render. Treat them as a clear finding when they appear in body text, not as a stylistic judgment call. Forward references to later sections are fine as plain pointers, not as asides to the reader. The same rule covers sentences that narrate the writing's own reasoning or announce that the writer is about to be careful or precise ("What that changes is worth being precise about.", "I want to be exact here.", "Before answering, it helps to separate two questions."). State the point instead.

## Plain, literal language

- **Use plain, literal language** (`G.plain-language`). The habits below make prose read as machine-written without adding meaning. The fix for each is the plain version. Each has an exception for genuinely precise usage. Scan for the sample words that these rules name even when nothing reads wrong, since a reviewer with the writer's habit may not notice them.
  - **No invented compounds or verbs-as-nouns** (`G.no-coinages`). Do not join domain words into hyphenated modifiers used as if they were established terms ("review-flattening", "new-entity creation", "data-integrity gating"), and do not turn a verb into a noun ("the delegate's own clear", "a full delete", "the ask"), including the count nouns from systems jargon "a write", "a read", "a create", and "a delete" (as in "each write", "the failed write", "repeat the create", or "reads and writes"). Rewrite to the verb or phrase that the coinage compressed: "review-flattening" -> "flattening the review"; "new-entity creation" -> "creating a new entity"; "data-integrity gating" -> "blocking changes that fail the integrity check"; "the delegate's own clear" -> "the delegate's own call to clear it"; "a full delete" -> "deleting all records"; "the ask" -> "the request"; "a write" -> "a write request" (or "a write operation"). The same pattern covers noun phrases coined ad hoc to name a specific artifact, document, or table, with or without a hyphen ("data sources table" and "track changes file" are the same coinages unhyphenated). Describe what the artifact is instead: "track-changes file" -> "the accompanying PDF" (or "the latexdiff PDF"); "data-sources table" -> "a table listing all data sources". Dropping the hyphen is not enough, and the **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`) participle exception does not cover an invented gerund compound. The pattern also covers an abstract label coined by attaching a problem-noun (e.g., paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, inversion) to a domain word and then using it as if it were established ("supervision paradox", "acceleration trap", "workload creep"). State the phenomenon in a clause instead ("workload creep" -> "the workload grew with each release"), or, when the text genuinely introduces a construct, define it on first use and keep the definition (see **Define terms on first use, then reuse exactly** (`G.define-once`)). Keep established terms and literal code, API, or config identifiers as written ("foreign-key constraint", a `user_secrets` key, a `git fetch`).
  - **Plain word over a colorful or insider synonym** (`G.plain-words`). Replace a vivid or niche word with its plain equivalent when the vivid word adds no precision: create not "mint", show or report not "surface", combine not "weave", guide not "shepherd", merge not "land", coordinate not "orchestrate", enable not "unlock", is in or is defined in not "lives in", structure, format, or pattern not the noun "shape", structure or influence not the verb "shape", publish or create not "cut". Replace "shape" with the property that it stands for, as in "the catalog's shape" -> "the catalog's structure", "a sentence of this shape" -> "a sentence with this pattern", "how the rules are shaped" -> "how the rules are structured", and "factors that shape adoption" -> "factors that influence adoption". Replace "cut" with the act that it stands for, as in "cut a release" -> "publish a release" and "cut a new reference from this run" -> "create a new reference from this run". When "gate" or "checkpoint" vaguely stands for checks, name what must pass and, when it matters, before which action. Examples include "the linter reported errors", "the integration tests must pass before committing", and "six commits, each ending at a green checkpoint" -> "six commits, and `gradle build` passes after each". Choose wording supported by the context. Keep the word only for a distinction that the plain word loses: "exercise" for driving inputs through a specific code path, not for "run"; "seam" for a Feathers seam, not for any interface; "shape" for a geometric form, the form of a statistical distribution, or the dimensions of an array, not for the structure of a document or a rule; "cut" for removing or shortening, not for creating or publishing; "checkpoint" for a saved state that a process resumes from (e.g., a model checkpoint or a database checkpoint), not for a point at which checks pass. These words are a sample of an open pattern, not a closed list.
  - **Use figurative language with a clear purpose** (`G.no-figurative-language`). Replace idioms and clichés when they obscure or exaggerate the claim, or repeat a stock formula without contributing meaning, as in "low-hanging fruit" -> "the easiest improvements", "the change did not move the needle" -> "the change did not lower the failure rate", "the choice boils down to cost" -> "the choice depends mainly on cost", "caching is a double-edged sword" -> "caching lowers latency but can serve stale data", and "at the end of the day, the tests must pass" -> "the tests must pass". Replace metaphorical framing when imagery substitutes for the relation that the reader needs, as in "the data tells a story of declining use" -> "the data shows that use declined" and "X is the backbone of Y" -> "Y depends on X". Familiar metaphors and conventional abstract framing can stay when they communicate the intended relation or emphasis clearly. Do not personify. Personification gives a mental state, a duty, or a merit to a subject that is not a person or a group of people: code, a model, a test, data, a document, a section, a sentence, a punctuation mark, a tool, a method, a rule, or an abstract noun. The finding is in the predicate, not the subject. The predicate states a desire ("the model wants to" -> "the model is trained to"; "the sentence wants a comma" -> "the sentence needs a comma"), a feeling ("the test is unhappy" -> "the test fails"; "the model struggles with long inputs" -> "accuracy drops on long inputs"), a belief or expectation ("the rule expects the reviewer to" -> "the rule requires that the reviewer"), or a duty or merit ("a period does the job" -> "a period is enough"; "earn its place" is under **Phrases to avoid** (`G.phrases-to-avoid`)). The fix names the mechanism or the observable behavior. A verb that names a defined behavior of the subject is literal (a function returns, a test fails, a parser expects a token, a query needs an index, a socket listens, a model learns), and so is the metonymy that names a document for its authors (the paper argues, the study finds, Section 3 shows). Exceptions: established technical terms with a metaphorical etymology are literal domain vocabulary (e.g., tree, branch, thread, stream, pipeline, fork, deadlock, mock, stub); and one analogy that genuinely helps a reader understand an unfamiliar concept is fine when introduced once, marked as an analogy, and not continued through the text.

## Self-check before presenting text

Before presenting any written or edited text, apply **Judge the passage before changing it** (`G.context-first`) to these checks. They identify candidates for inspection, not a target number of edits:

1. **Word-level scan** (`G.restricted-words`, `G.catalog-precedence`). Scan for the catalog's word-level patterns (**"Delve" and friends**, **"Tapestry" and "Landscape"**, **"Quietly" and other magic adverbs**) and assess their function in context before proposing a replacement, keeping vocabulary that these rules explicitly leave unrestricted (see **Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`)). Count restricted words (per the table above). If more than 2 to 3 appear in a single paragraph, inspect whether they obscure or inflate the claim. Do not replace an apt word merely to reduce the count.
2. **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Check each em-dash. Replace it where a period or comma would read better. As a secondary signal, more than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance. Exclude from any count: em-dashes inside quoted source material, and glyphs that are not em-dashes at all, such as en dashes in numeric or page ranges (`pp. 12–18`), minus signs in math, and hyphens or `--` sequences inside code listings. Also exclude the two structural exceptions noted in the **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) rule (appositives that contain commas, and parentheticals that contain parentheses).
3. **Colons** (`G.colons`). Examine each colon. Use a period where it clarifies the relation without losing the setup or emphasis. Watch especially for colons substituting for em-dashes or parentheses, and for a colon followed by a list in paragraph after paragraph. As a secondary signal, more than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
4. **Introducer punctuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). For every sentence immediately followed by an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), a bulleted block, or a paragraph that grammatically continues or elaborates it, verify that the introducer ends with `:`, not `.`. AI text systematically defaults to `.` here. The `:` is correct and does not count toward the colon signal.
5. **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). For every colon in running prose, check whether the text that follows is a complete sentence. If it is, the first word must be capitalized. If it is a fragment, phrase, single word, or list, keep the first word lowercase. Fix mismatches. For a colon introducing an enumerated series of independent clauses, keep the first word lowercase (it is a list) and prefer splitting three or more such clauses into sentences (see `G.no-list-cramming`).
6. **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Examine each semicolon. Consider a period when it makes the relation easier to read. The fact that both clauses can stand alone is not enough to require a change. Reserve semicolons for genuine list disambiguation and for tightly coupled independent clauses where the link adds meaning that a period would lose. As a secondary signal, more than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
7. **Combined pause-punctuation signal** (`G.pause-mark-count`). After judging em-dashes, colons, and semicolons individually, count the three together per page-equivalent as a secondary check. A page within each individual ceiling can still be over-punctuated (3 + 2 + 2 = 7 mid-sentence pause marks). Around 5 or fewer per page in running prose is a reasonable target. Prefer a period when it improves the passage; do not change punctuation solely to lower the count.
8. **Sentence length variance** (`G.sentence-length`). Inspect runs of three sentences within 5 words of each other in length for monotonous rhythm. Preserve natural phrasing and useful parallelism; a similar word count alone requires no edit.
9. **Opening / closing patterns** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`, `G.no-formulaic-closings`). Verify that no section starts with a formulaic opener, with an announcement of what follows ("Two constraints shape the design."), or with a paraphrase of the question being answered. Verify that no section ends with "Overall, ..." or "In summary, ...". When `In summary,` / `In conclusion,` / `Overall,` does appear, confirm that it introduces a real synthesis (an enumerated list distilling earlier prose, or a synthesis across multiple data points) rather than restating a single preceding paragraph.
10. **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). Inspect repeated present-participle openings for monotony or an unclear actor. Rewrite the problematic opening, not every occurrence above a count.
11. **Hedging density** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). If a paragraph contains more than two hedging phrases (e.g., may, might, could, perhaps, to some extent), evaluate whether each is evidence-based. Remove the hedges that are not.
12. **Passive voice** (`G.active-voice`). If more than one-third of sentences in a paragraph are passive, inspect whether the actors or actions are obscured. Prefer active voice when it improves the passage; preserve a passive that suits its focus.
13. **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). Count single-sentence paragraphs per page, and count paragraphs that are a bare fragment. More than one or two on a page, or any run of three or more in a row, is a flag. Fold each into a neighbor unless it has a structural purpose that absorbing it would lose, such as opening a section, introducing a list or figure, marking a section transition, or carrying a stand-alone topic sentence.
14. **American English spelling** (`G.american-english`). Search for common British variants: analyse, behaviour, organisation, modelling, colour, favour, centre, defence, licence. Replace with American equivalents.
15. **Redundancy check** (`G.refer-back`). Scan for definitions, points, or findings stated in full more than once. Replace duplicated content with a reference back to where it is first stated. One-sentence reminders are fine. Restated paragraphs are not.
16. **Terminology consistency** (`G.one-term`). Verify that each key concept is referred to by the same term throughout. Standardize cases where synonyms are used interchangeably for the same concept.
17. **Hyphenation audit** (`G.compound-hyphens`). Scan for hyphenated noun-noun stacks placed before another noun (e.g., "code-generation benchmarks", "code-translation performance", "data-collection process"). Drop the hyphen unless one element is a participle that would otherwise re-attach as a verb (e.g., "instruction-following ability" stays).
18. **Parenthetical-list signaling** (`G.parenthetical-lists`, `G.one-example-signal`). Scan for `(X, Y, Z)` patterns with 2+ comma-separated items. Verify that each is signaled as examples (`e.g.,`) or as a definition/full enumeration (`i.e.,`). If neither applies and the list is not obviously exhaustive, add `e.g.,`. Also flag any parenthetical that mixes `e.g.,` with `etc.`, or that uses `etc.` while the rest of the document signals examples with `e.g.,`. Pick one signal per document and apply it consistently. Skip abbreviation expansions such as "large language models (LLMs)".
19. **Concision** (`G.sentence-padding`, `G.paragraph-padding`). Scan for sentence-level padding (multi-word filler such as "in order to", "due to the fact that", "has the ability to"; empty lead-ins such as "it is important to note that"; redundant doublets such as "end result") and replace or delete it. Then scan each paragraph for windup sentences (a section-opening paragraph that states the section's claim or orients the reader across its subsections is not one) and for sentences that restate a predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, or evidence, and cut them. Count generic-truth sentences, aphorisms, and evaluative sentences. More than one in a paragraph, or more than two or three per page-equivalent, is filler. Apply the subtractive test. If deleting a word, clause, or sentence costs no meaning, emphasis, or precision, delete it.
20. **List-cramming** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Scan for any single sentence that packs three or more independent clauses as a run-in series (typically a colon or dash, then semicolon-joined clauses, each often with its own citation). Break it into separate sentences, or reduce the items to short parallel fragments so a colon introduces a true list. Two linked clauses, or a colon introducing one payoff clause, do not count.
21. **Plain, literal language** (`G.no-coinages`, `G.plain-words`, `G.no-figurative-language`). Scan for (a) invented hyphenated compounds and verbs-used-as-nouns (including coinages naming a specific artifact or document, hyphenated or not, such as "track-changes file" / "data sources table", the bare count nouns "a write", "a read", "a delete", and abstract labels built from a domain word plus a problem-noun such as paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, or inversion), (b) colorful or insider synonyms that have a plainer equivalent, including "gate" or "checkpoint" used instead of naming what must pass, and (c) idioms, metaphorical framing, and personification, that is, a desire, feeling, belief, duty, or merit predicated of code, a model, data, a document, a sentence, a punctuation mark, a tool, a method, or a rule. Treat each as a candidate and apply `G.context-first`. Rewrite when the phrase obscures a mechanism, exaggerates a claim, or contributes no meaning. Keep clear conventional framing, an apt metaphor, an established term, and literal code/API/config identifiers. A sample-word match alone is not a finding.
22. **Literal em-dash glyphs** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). Search for the literal `—` (U+2014). Replace with `---` in LaTeX; with `:`, `-`, or a table in Markdown/plain text. Exclude en dashes in ranges, minus signs, and `—` inside quotes or code.
23. **Example/restatement connectives** (`G.connectives`). For each em-dash or colon flagged under `G.em-dashes` and `G.colons`, check what it introduces before collapsing it to a period or comma. If it introduces an example, prefer such as, e.g., for example, or including. If it introduces a restatement or definition, prefer i.e., that is, in other words, or namely. Leave alone a mark that sets up a payoff clause or introduces a true list, and keep the example signal consistent per `G.parenthetical-lists`.
24. **Reference** (`G.anchor-pronouns`, `G.summarizing-nouns`, `G.definite-article`, `G.stand-ins`). For each sentence-initial *This*, *These*, *That*, or *It* and each *such/this/these* + noun, apply the "[noun] just mentioned" test. Attach or repeat the noun where the reference is unclear, and replace a summarizing noun that names nothing that the text listed. For each *the* + noun at first mention, ask "which one?" and drop the definite when the preceding text cannot answer. Replace a stand-in word (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *respectively*, *do so*) when its referent is unclear or the lookup obstructs reading. Keep immediate, understandable references. Skip dummy *it*.
25. **Clause boundaries** (`G.keep-that`, `G.whose-on-things`). In formal prose, restore *that* between two adjacent noun phrases and after a reporting verb when the following noun phrase is a clause subject. Leave fixed heads, quoted text, and deliberately informal text alone. For each *whose* with a thing as its head, restate the condition with *with*, a participle or verb on the head, or a clause with the property as its subject, and keep the restatement when it loses nothing. As a secondary signal, more than one *whose* per page-equivalent of running prose flags the habit.
26. **Announced counts** (`G.no-announced-counts`). Scan for sentences that state how many items follow. Keep the count where a later sentence refers back to it, or where the items are an enumerated specification; a checklist; the conditions, phases, or research questions of a study; or a contribution list. Delete it elsewhere and state the first item.
27. **Prose listicles** (`G.no-prose-listicle`). Scan for consecutive paragraphs opened by ordinals that number an unmarked list. Set the material as a real list, or drop the ordinals and open each paragraph with its claim. Ordinals marking a short sequence within one paragraph are correct and stay.
28. **Consequence connectives** (`G.consequence-connectives`). Delete every "So" that opens a sentence in formal prose, and move a sentence-initial "Therefore," or "Thus," into mid-position. Count so, therefore, thus, hence, consequently, and as a result together per page-equivalent, and read any run of consecutive sentences that use one of them. Above two or three per page, or where one word carries nearly all of them at that density, delete the connectives that the content order already implies, recast with because or since where the cause reads better after the effect, and match the rest to the step. Substitute one word for another last. Leave parallelism across items of the same kind alone. Skip the intensifier, "so that", the correlative "so ... that", and quoted material.
29. **Empty nouns and verbs** (`G.be-concrete`). Scan for thing, stuff, aspect, element, factor, area, item, component, piece, part, issue, concept, and entity as a head noun, and for a number of, various, certain, and several. First determine whether the noun is an empty placeholder or a useful abstraction, frame, or summary. Use a specific noun when the reader needs that referent. If an essential referent cannot be determined, record the finding under author judgment rather than guessing. Do not replace a purpose statement with a list of topics. Apply the same test to an empty verb phrase. In revision descriptions, scan for "moved" and "shifted" and name the actual change or current behavior, using only what the source establishes. Keep version history when it helps comparison, upgrading, reproduction, or understanding a change. Keep actual relocation with a named source and destination, physical movement, and established technical senses. Keep the generic noun where it contrasts with another category, carries an established technical sense, or names a category that the text defined.
30. **Context and purpose** (`G.context-first`). Read the surrounding paragraph, state the concrete benefit of a proposed stylistic edit, and check that it preserves purpose, logical relations, scope, emphasis, and voice. Keep the original when the difference is only a preference for shorter, more literal, or more explicit wording. Apply explicit conventions as conventions rather than labeling every departure as AI slop.

## AI Writing Tropes to Avoid

The catalog below is sourced from [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by [ossama.is](https://ossama.is) and inlined here so co-authors and offline tooling can read it without re-fetching. The skill's runtime fetches the live catalog on every run. This inlined copy is for reading by humans and other Agent Skills clients.

### Negative parallelism

`consistent` · Sentence Structure

El Classico, the "It's not X -- it's Y" pattern. The single most commonly identified AI writing tell. Man I f*cking hate it. The model uses this to create false profundity by framing everything as a surprise. Once can be effective; TEN in a blog post is a genuine insult to the reader.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "It's not bold. It's backwards."
- "The question isn't whether to optimize. The question is when to stop."
- "The real issue isn't technical. The real issue is organizational."

---

### Em-dash addiction

`consistent` · Formatting

Compulsive overuse of em dashes for dramatic pauses, parenthetical asides and pivot points. A human writer might use 2-3 per piece (and naturally); AI will use a lot more.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The problem -- and this is the part nobody talks about -- is systemic."
- "The tinkerer spirit didn't die of natural causes -- it was bought out."
- "Not recklessly, not completely -- but enough -- enough to matter."

---

### Short punchy fragments

`rising` · Paragraph Structure

Excessive use of very short sentences or sentence fragments as standalone paragraphs for MANUFACTURED EMPHASIS. RLHF training has pushed models toward "writing for readability" aimed at the lowest common denominator: one thought per sentence, no mental state-keeping required. It's an inhuman style and no real person writes first drafts this way because it doesn't match how humans think or speak.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest."
- "These weren't just products. The software side matched. Then it professionalised. But I adapted."
- "Flaky tests. A twenty-minute build. Waiting two days for review. A staging environment that's broken half the time."

---

### Reasoning leak

`new` · Composition

Unpromptedly narrating what the text or the model itself is doing, deciding, planning, or about to do, instead of just doing it and producing the output. The reader gets a voiceover of the writing's own moves and the model's deliberation. Literal chain-of-thought residue that has no place in the final output, serving solely to pollute and bloat the content.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "What that changes in the design is smaller than it might appear, and what it changes is worth being precise about."
- "I want to be exact about my own role here."
- "I should be clear that none of this fell on me."

---

### Premise stacking

`new` · Composition

A point (often, but not only, a question) preceded by a paragraph of its own evidence, so by the time the model spits it out it has already been made two or three times over. Increasingly common and very much linked to reasoning leaks.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Is next-day delivery available in this region, and what's the current rollout status? One internal doc says it launched in the spring. Another says it's still being tested. A teammate mentioned it was paused in a message last month. Every other region has a clear answer, and if this one doesn't, that's a conversation with the partner rather than something we can fix ourselves."
- "Now imagine if this was simply: Is next-day delivery available in this region?"

---

### Preamble (announce-then-answer)

`new` · Composition

Opening with what the output is about to do, a preface to the point, or a restatement of the prompt, instead of delivering the point. Includes structural announcers that name the count or shape of what follows ("Two constraints shape the design"), throat-clearing frames ("The more important point is..."), and paraphrasing the question back. The sentence sets up the answer instead of being the answer. Sits in the signposting family with compulsive-counting (states the number) and enumerated-prose (the "The first... The second..." delivery); this is the announcer that precedes them.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Two constraints shape the design."
- "Two continuations are worth supporting, and they serve different situations."
- "The more important point is where the decision itself sits."

---

### Grandiose stakes inflation

`rising` · Tone

Everything is the most important thing ever. AI inflates the stakes of every argument to world-historical significance. A blog post about API pricing becomes a meditation on the fate of civilisation.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "This will fundamentally reshape how we think about everything."
- "the next decade of software won't look like a smarter version of today. It will look like something entirely new"

---

### Compulsive counting

`new` · Tone

Ever since we bullied models for not being able to count they have been building toward this moment. Claude et al. are super excited to share their newfound ability to count by stating the exact number of items before listing them, as if getting the count right were itself the achievement.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Five things we wish to discuss"
- "Two joints, one beam rated for the load, one measurement and one calculation is enough to answer all four."
- "Four reasons why this will work"

---

### Invented concept labels

`rising` · Tone

AI clusters invented compound labels that sound analytical without being grounded. It appends abstract problem-nouns (paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, inversion) to domain words -- "supervision paradox", "acceleration trap", "workload creep" -- and uses them as if they're established, rigorously defined terms. They function as rhetorical shorthand: name a thing, skip the argument. Multiple such labels in the same piece is a strong signal of AI slop.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "the supervision paradox"
- "the acceleration trap"
- "workload creep"

---

### Rule of Three pattern

`consistent` · Sentence Structure

Overuse of the rule-of-three pattern, often extended to four or five. A single tricolon is elegant; three back-to-back tricolons (tri-tricolons) are a pattern recognition failure.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Products impress people; platforms empower them. Products solve problems; platforms create worlds. Products scale linearly; platforms scale exponentially."
- "These are design flaws, not syntax errors. They compile. They might even work. But they're code smells."
- "Measure ticket throughput and you get ... Measure velocity and you get ... Measure code coverage and you get ..."

---

### Belaboring the unnecessary

`new` · Composition

Stating a minor or uncontroversial point just to defend it as if anticipating an objection nobody was going to raise.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "We are setting this out in full rather than quietly changing the recommendation, because the failure mode is the reason it matters."
- "I don't mean any of that as cynicism about players."
- "We would rather not put a number on the load the beam can carry until the material testing is complete, because the figure is set almost entirely by three factors that sit with the supplier rather than with us."

---

### "Quietly" and other magic adverbs

`consistent` · Word Choice

Overuse of "quietly" and similar adverbs to convey subtle importance or understated power. AI reaches for these adverbs to make mundane descriptions feel significant. Also includes: "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "arguably", and the standalone construction "unusually well [X]".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "quietly orchestrating workflows, decisions, and interactions"
- "the one that quietly suffocates everything else"
- "a quiet intelligence behind it"

---

### The Tie-Back

`consistent` · Composition

Closing by restating the answer and looping it back to the original question, instead of stopping once the answer is given. The reply has already delivered the point, then bolts a summary of itself back onto the ask ("So, to answer your question, X does Y").

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "So, to answer your question: yes, the employee can be added to the app."
- "In short, this gives you everything you need to ship."
- "To bring it back to what you asked, the design runs on both systems."

---

### Vague attributions

`consistent` · Tone

Attributing claims to unnamed authorities instead of being specific. AI loves to invoke "experts", "observers", "industry reports", and "several publications" without naming anyone. It also inflates the quantity of sources -- presenting what one person said as a widely held view, or writing "several publications have cited" when it means two. If you can't name the expert, you don't have a source.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Experts argue that this approach has significant drawbacks."
- "Industry reports suggest that adoption is accelerating."
- "Observers have cited the initiative as a turning point."

---

### Self-echo

`new` · Composition

The model reuses one of its own words or phrases from earlier in the same document as if paying it off, when it's really the same narrow vocabulary surfacing again under sustained topic pressure.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "quietly become the real source of truth ... nothing you've ever believed can quietly disappear"
- "what you believed last week isn't there to check anymore ... nothing you've ever believed can quietly disappear"
- "wearing the same italics ... wearing a different costume"

---

### Quotable one-liners

`new` · Tone

A standalone line made to sound quotable but carries no actual information essentially pure slide bait. The line is built to be pulled out and read alone (with zero context) as if it were wisdom. Read the examples and tell me what they actually mean. NOTHING.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Story points are a planning tool with no fixed unit."
- "Every metric that rewards volume punishes leverage."
- "When everything gets maximum emphasis, nothing has any."

---

### Forced figurative language

`new` · Tone

A forced simile or coined metaphor reached for because it sounds clever rather than because it clarifies anything. Nobody would use this in real life. Opus 5 takes it further and takes a word from your prompt and repurpose it as a metaphor for something totally unrelated.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Using them as a productivity measure is like tracking your weight loss with a scale that you also control the calibration on."

---

### Never-ending conclusion

`new` · Composition

The ending stacks clause after clause instead of landing one point like the model is reluctant to actually stop.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "But you can't optimize what you're mismeasuring, and a wrong metric is worse than no metric because it actively steers. If you only change one thing: stop measuring individuals by output volume, start measuring the system's ability to deliver working software, and ask your engineers what's in the way. The last one is free, and it will tell you more in an afternoon than a quarter of velocity charts."

---

### Comma-clipped trailing phrase

`new` · Sentence Structure

A short tail hung off a comma to close a sentence instead of landing the point directly. Either a clipped clause finishing the thought sideways or sometimes it's a bare noun or short phrase tacked on as an afterthought. Increasingly common.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "above the content, and save."
- "asked forty times, mentoring."

---

### Synonym cycling

`new` · Word Choice

Refusing to repeat the same noun twice, cycling through synonyms for one referent instead. A dashboard becomes an interface, then a portal, then the analytics hub, all in the same paragraph. Just use one word, stop flexing your vocabulary, it's pointless and you're losing the reader.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "the dashboard ... the interface ... the portal ... the analytics hub"

---

### Appeal to familiarity

`new` · Tone

Asserting canonical or well-known status for a claim, without evidence, to borrow the weight of consensus: "a classic," "famously," "notoriously," "as we all know." The unnamed authority is the reader's own supposed prior knowledge instead of an outside expert.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "A classic,"

---

### Promotional language

`new` · Tone

Almost all AI writing now reads like marketing copy or a travel brochure instead of factual prose, attempting to sell the subject instead of describing it.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "an all-in-one solution that unlocks unprecedented productivity for teams of any size"
- "a seamless experience that elevates every part of your workflow"

---

### "Where / What / Why" Headers

`new` · Formatting

Headings built on a Wh-word, now the default shape the model reaches for whenever it has to name a section, whether an article heading or a slide title. A serious tell on its own, independent of what the content under the heading actually says.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Where the market is stuck today"
- "What we do differently"
- "What it's worth to you"

---

### "Where it actually lives"

`new` · Word Choice

Framing the true location or source of something as a physical inhabitance, as a stand-in for a direct answer.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "where the complexity actually lives"

---

### Collaborative communication

`consistent` · Tone

Claude seems to be speaking French now, who is we? Why does a document that I authored but formatted need its "I"s switched to "we"s? Very contextual, some authors genuinely use we, but it does signal a loss of personal voice, especially in personal material.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "We're now equipped to handle whatever comes next."
- "This gives us a much clearer picture of what's really going on."
- "As we move forward, our focus shifts to execution."

---

### "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."

`consistent` · Sentence Structure

Like negative parallelism but tripled up. AI builds tension by negating two or more things before revealing the actual point. Creates a false sense of narrowing down to the truth.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Not a bug. Not a feature. A fundamental design flaw."
- "Not ten. Not fifty. Five hundred and twenty-three lint violations across 67 files."
- "not recklessly, not completely, but enough"

---

### "Here's the kicker"

`consistent` · Tone

False suspense transitions that promise a revelation but deliver a point that did NOT need the buildup. The model uses these phrases to manufacture drama before an otherwise completely unremarkable observation LOL.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Here's the thing about AI adoption."
- "Here's what most people miss about this."
- "But here's the catch."

---

### Fractal summaries

`rising` · Composition

"What I'm going to tell you; what I'm telling you; what I just told you" applied at every level of the document. Every subsection gets a summary. Every section gets a summary. The document itself gets a summary.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "In this section, we'll explore... [3000 words later] ...as we've seen in this section."
- "A conclusion that restates every point already made in the previous 3000 words"
- "Opens with 'The Paradox of AI Growth', closes with 'The paradox is solvable'"

---

### Excessive enumeration

`rising` · Paragraph Structure

Numbered or labeled points dressed up as continuous prose. The model writes what is essentially a listicle but wraps each point in a paragraph that starts with "The first... The second... The third..." to disguise the format. Perhaps you told it to stop generating lists and it decided to do this instead.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The first wall is the absence of a free, scoped API... The second wall is the lack of delegated access... The third wall is the absence of scoped permissions..."
- "The second takeaway is that... The third takeaway is that... The fourth takeaway is that..."

---

### Title case headings

`consistent` · Formatting

Capitalising every word in a heading instead of just the first word and proper nouns. Should've captured this before but I wasn't working on slides and presentation material as frequently to realise!

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Understanding The Impact Of Modern Technology On Society"

---

### "Tapestry" and "Landscape"

`consistent` · Word Choice

Overuse of ornate or grandiose nouns where simpler words would do. "Tapestry" is used to describe anything interconnected. "Landscape" is used to describe any field or domain. Other offenders: "paradigm", "synergy", "ecosystem", "framework", "load-bearing" (for important), "gated" (for restricted or conditional). Although some words cycle out of fashion for the models, new ones get introduced e.g. load-bearing, gated, paradigm.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The rich tapestry of human experience..."
- "Navigating the complex landscape of modern AI..."
- "The ever-evolving landscape of technology..."

---

### "The X? A Y."

`fading` · Sentence Structure

Self-posed rhetorical questions answered immediately in the next sentence or clause. The model asks a question nobody was asking, then answers it for dramatic effect. Thinks this is the epitome of great writing.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The result? Devastating."
- "The worst part? Nobody saw it coming."
- "The scary part? This attack vector is perfect for developers."

---

### Anaphora abuse

`fading` · Sentence Structure

Repeating the same sentence opening multiple times in quick succession.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "They assume that users will pay... They assume that developers will build... They assume that ecosystems will emerge... They assume that..."
- "They could expose... They could offer... They could provide... They could create... They could let... They could unlock..."
- "They have built engines, but not vehicles. They have built power, but not leverage. They have built walls, but not doors."

---

### Bold-first bullets

`fading` · Formatting

Every bullet point or list item starts with a bolded phrase or sentence. Extremely common in Claude and ChatGPT markdown output. Almost nobody formats lists this way when writing by hand. It's a telltale sign of AI-generated documentation and blog posts and README files (especially with emojis). Eh, this one isn't so bad but it's an immediate spot when scanning a doc, most people use skills to format documents in a specific way so it is fading.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Every single bullet point begins with a bold keyword."
- "**Security**: Environment-based configuration with..."
- "**Performance**: Lazy loading of expensive resources..."

---

### "Think of it as..."

`fading` · Tone

The patronizing analogy. AI constantly reaches for "Think of it as..." or "It's like a..." to simplify concepts. The model defaults to teacher mode and assumes the reader needs a metaphor to understand anything. Often produces analogies that are less clear than the original concept. I kinda miss this, now Claude speaks to you in a completely unintelligible language.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Think of it like a highway system for data."
- "Imagine it as a Swiss Army knife for your codebase."

---

### Unicode decoration

`fading` · Formatting

Use of unicode arrows (->), smart/curly quotes, and other special characters that can't be easily typed on a standard keyboard. Real writers typing in a text editor produce straight quotes and -> or =>. Claude in particular loves the -> arrow.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Input → Processing → Output"
- "This leads to better outcomes → which means higher engagement"
- "“Smart quotes” instead of straight "quotes" that you’d actually type"

---

### Rapid-fire historical analogies

`fading` · Composition

ESPECIALLY COMMON IN TECHNICAL WRITING. Rapid-fire listing of historical companies or tech revolutions to build false authority.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Apple didn't build Uber. Facebook didn't build Spotify. Stripe didn't build Shopify. AWS didn't build Airbnb."
- "Every major technological shift -- the web, mobile, social, cloud -- followed the same pattern."

---

### "Imagine a world where..."

`fading` · Tone

The classic AI invitation to futurism. To sell the argument usually begins with "Imagine" followed by a list of wonderful things that will happen if the reader agrees with the premise.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Imagine a world where every tool you use -- your calendar, your inbox, your documents, your CRM, your code editor -- has a quiet intelligence behind it..."

---

### False vulnerability

`fading` · Tone

Simulated self-awareness or honesty that reads as performative. The model pretends to break the fourth wall or admit a bias, creating a false sense of authenticity. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable; AI vulnerability is polished and risk-free!!!!

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "And yes, I'm openly in love with the platform model"
- "And yes, since we're being honest: I'm looking at you, OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta"
- "This is not a rant; it's a diagnosis"

---

### False ranges

`fading` · Sentence Structure

Using "from X to Y" constructions where X and Y aren't on any real scale. In legitimate use, "from X to Y" implies a spectrum with a meaningful middle. AI uses it as a jarring way to list two loosely related things.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "From innovation to implementation to cultural transformation."
- "From the singularity of the Big Bang to the grand cosmic web."
- "From problem-solving and tool-making to scientific discovery, artistic expression, and technological innovation."

---

### The "Serves As" dodge

`fading` · Word Choice

Replacing simple "is" or "are" with pompous alternatives like "serves as", "stands as", "marks", or "represents". AI avoids basic copulas because its repetition penalty pushes it toward fancier constructions (I've studied this!).

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The building serves as a reminder of the city's heritage."
- "Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space for contemporary art."
- "The station marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional transit."

---

### One-point dilution

`fading` · Composition

Making a single argument and restating it in 10 different ways across thousands of words. The model pads a simple thesis to feel "comprehensive" by rephrasing the same idea with different metaphors, examples, and framings. An 800-word argument becomes 4000 words of circular repetition.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The same point, restated eight ways across 4000 words."
- "Each section rephrases the thesis with a different metaphor but adds nothing new"

---

### Content duplication

`fading` · Composition

Repeating entire sections or paragraphs verbatim within the same piece. This happens when the model loses track of what it has already written, especially in longer pieces. A dead giveaway of unedited AI output. Less common nowadays thanks to 1M context windows.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "The same section appeared twice, word-for-word identical."
- "Paragraph 3 and paragraph 17 are the same sentence reworded"

---

### "Delve" and friends

`fading` · Word Choice

Used to be the most infamous AI tell. "Delve" went from an uncommon English word to appearing in a staggering percentage of AI-generated text. Part of a family of overused AI vocabulary including "certainly", "utilize", "leverage" (as a verb), "robust", "streamline", and "harness".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Let's delve into the details..."
- "Delving deeper into this topic..."
- "We certainly need to leverage these robust frameworks..."

---

### "It's worth noting"

`fading` · Sentence Structure

Filler transitions that signal nothing. AI uses these phrases to introduce new points without actually connecting them to the previous argument. Also includes: "It bears mentioning", "Importantly", "Interestingly", "Notably".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "It's worth noting that this approach has limitations."
- "Importantly, we must consider the broader implications."
- "Interestingly, this pattern repeats across industries."

---

### "Let's break this down"

`fading` · Tone

The pedagogical voice that assumes the reader needs hand-holding. AI defaults to a teacher-student dynamic even when writing for expert audiences. Also includes: "Let's unpack this", "Let's explore", "Let's dive in".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Let's break this down step by step."
- "Let's unpack what this really means."
- "Let's explore this idea further."

---

### Superficial analyses

`fading` · Sentence Structure

Adding ("-ing") onto the end of a sentence to inject shallow analysis that says nothing. The model attaches significance, legacy, or broader meaning to mundane facts using phrases like "highlighting its importance", "reflecting broader trends", or "contributing to the development of...".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "contributing to the region's rich cultural heritage"
- "This etymology highlights the enduring legacy of the community's resistance and the transformative power of unity in shaping its identity."
- "underscoring its role as a dynamic hub of activity and culture"

---

### "Despite its challenges..."

`fading` · Composition

The rigid formula where AI acknowledges problems only to immediately dismiss them. Always follows the same flow: "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges..." then ends with "Despite these challenges, [optimistic conclusion].".

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "Despite these challenges, the initiative continues to thrive."
- "Despite its industrial and residential prosperity, Goonga faces challenges typical of urban areas."
- "Despite their promising applications, pyroelectric materials face several challenges that must be addressed for broader adoption."

---

### Signposted conclusion

`consistent` · Composition

Explicitly announcing the conclusion with "In conclusion", "To sum up", or "In summary". Competent writing doesn't need to tell you it's concluding, it's obvious and the reader can feel it. AI signals its structural moves because it's following a template, not writing organically, it doesn't know when its actually going to end.

**Avoid patterns like:**

- "In conclusion, the future of AI depends on..."
- "To sum up, we've explored three key themes..."
- "In summary, the evidence suggests..."

---

Remember: any of these patterns used once might be fine. The problem is when
multiple tropes appear together or when a single trope is used repeatedly.
Write like a human: varied, imperfect, specific.
